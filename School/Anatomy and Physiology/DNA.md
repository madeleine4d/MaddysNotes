### Basics of DNA
- Double helix structure
- 5’ to 3’ ends
	- 5’ is the side of the monomer with the phosphate
	- because the phosphate connects to the 5’ carbon and 3’ carbon of the sugar.
- both helices run in opposite directions (one 5’ to 3’ and the other 3’ to 5’)
- Alanine (A), Tyrosine (T), Cyanine (C), and Guanine (G) are called base pairs
    - A forms 2 [[H-bonds]] to T
    - C forms 3 [[H-bonds]] to G
    - This is built in redundancy: if one base pair is damaged or removed the other member of the pair can be used to replace it
- DNA can replicated ([[DNA Replication]]) by splitting in half and replacing each missing base pair member, making two chains. This is the [[semi-conservative model]]

### DNA packing

![[Pasted image 20260204224532.png]]
#### basic structure
the most common primary structure is the B-form double helix which has a right hand twist. This form is stabilized by [[H-bonds]] and [[Van der Waals forces]] between the stacked bases.
#### supercoiling
dsDNA twists over its self like a twisted telephone cord. This takes a positive and negative from:
1. positive supercoiling occurs when the additional twist is in the same direction (has the same right handedness) as the DNA helix. This makes the DNA helix tighter and makes replication more difficult. 
2. negative supercoiling occurs when the additional twist has left handedness. This makes the DNA helix looser and more easily replicated. Most cellular DNA is in this form.

![[Pasted image 20260204223758.png]]

DNA supercoiling is regulated by Topoisomerase. Topoisomerases cut dsDNA allowing for it to be wound or unwound before it re-ligates the back bone.

#### Histones
DNA wraps around a core of eight histone proteins (two each of histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4) to form a structure called a **nucleosome**. Each nucleosome consists of approximately 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped around the histone core in 1.65 turns.

Between each nucleosome is a short segment of DNA, known as linker DNA, which is not wrapped around histones. A fifth histone protein, **H1**, binds to the linker DNA, helping to further stabilize the nucleosome structure.

Histones are positively charged and thus bind with the negatively charged DNA, neutralizing its charge. Formation of nucleosomes reduces the length of dsDNA by about 6 times.

![[Pasted image 20260204224403.png]]

#### higher order folding
Nucleosomes are formed into 30 mn fibers which reduces DNA length by a further 40 times.
![[Pasted image 20260204233119.png]]

DNA is further packaged into loop domains by anchor proteins which regulate [[gene]] expression and respond to environmental factors.

During [[cell division]], the DNA is compacted into discrete structures called **chromosomes**. This is achieved through further folding and coiling of the 30-nm fibers and loop domains into tightly pack chromatids.

- **Centromere**: The centromere is the region where two sister chromatids are held together. It also serves as the attachment point for spindle fibers during cell division. contains repetitive DNA, which is sometimes transcribed, and has a moderate mutation rate.
    
- **Telomeres**: Telomeres are repetitive sequences at the ends of chromosomes that protect them from degradation and prevent them from being mistaken for broken DNA. Telomeres play an important role in aging and cancer biology. Contains highly repetitive DNA, which is not transcribed, and has a high mutation rate.

- most other DNA is non-repetitive and much of it is transcribed. This DNA tends to have a lower mutation rate.
#### Chromosome Territories
each chromosome has a territory within the nucleus and these territories can be active and inactive.
- **Euchromatin**: Chromatin in its loosely packed form. Euchromatin is generally transcriptionally active.
    
- **Heterochromatin**: is more densely packed and is generally transcriptionally inactive. It is found in regions of the chromosome containing repetitive sequences, such as centromeres and telomeres.
